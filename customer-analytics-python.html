Project overview

This project analyzes 9,994 order line items from a US-based retail superstore (2014–2017) to understand sales patterns and identify which customers are most valuable, using Python for cleaning, exploration, and RFM (Recency, Frequency, Monetary) customer segmentation.

Business problem

Retail businesses often treat all customers the same, spending equally on retention and marketing across the board. Without knowing which customers are genuinely high-value versus which are drifting away, marketing budget gets wasted and at-risk high-value customers get missed.

Business questions

  • Which product categories and sub-categories actually drive sales — and which drive profit?
  • Are there sub-categories that sell well but lose money?
  • How does revenue trend month to month — is there seasonality?
  • Which customers are the most valuable, and which are at risk of churning?

Dataset overview

9,994 rows across 21 columns, covering order details, customer info, product category, region, sales, and profit for 793 unique customers and 1,850 unique products across 49 US states.

Data cleaning

Checked for missing values (none found) and duplicate rows (none found). Converted order and ship dates from text into proper date format so they could be used for time-based analysis and the recency calculation in RFM.

Analysis performed

1. Sales & profit by category

Technology leads in total sales, narrowly ahead of Furniture and Office Supplies.

2. Monthly sales trend

Sales spike sharply every November/December and trend upward year over year overall.

3. Most and least profitable sub-categories

Copiers, Phones, and Accessories are the most profitable. Tables and Bookcases actually lose money overall, despite generating sales.

4. RFM customer segmentation

Each of the 793 customers was scored on Recency (days since last order), Frequency (number of orders), and Monetary value (total spend), then grouped into five segments.

Distribution of all 793 customers across the five RFM segments.

Key findings

"Champions" are only 24% of customers (193 of 793) but generate $921,597 — roughly 40% of total revenue.
141 customers are "At Risk" (recently active but declining) and 55 are "Lost / Low Value" — together nearly 25% of the customer base, worth targeting before they're gone for good.
Tables and Bookcases are sold regularly but are net-negative on profit — likely due to steep discounting eating into margin.
Sales are strongly seasonal, spiking every November-December — useful for inventory and staffing planning.

Business impact

These findings let a business focus retention spend on the 24% of customers who generate the majority of revenue, flag at-risk high-value customers before they churn, and reconsider pricing or discount policy on chronically unprofitable products.

Recommendations

Customer retention

  • Build a loyalty or early-access program targeted specifically at "Champions" and "Loyal Customers" to protect the ~65% of revenue they generate.

Win-back campaigns

  • Target the 141 "At Risk" customers with a re-engagement offer before they move into the "Lost" segment.

Pricing review

  • Review discounting practices on Tables and Bookcases specifically — they may need a minimum-margin floor on discounts.

Inventory planning

  • Plan stock and staffing increases ahead of the recurring November-December demand spike.

Project outcome

Turned a raw, uncategorized transaction log into a segmented view of exactly which customers and products matter most — the kind of analysis a retail business could act on directly.

How to reproduce this

The full Python script is available to download below. It uses only Pandas and Matplotlib — no advanced libraries required. The core technique (RFM scoring with pd.qcut to split customers into quartiles) is a standard, widely-used approach for customer segmentation and works on any dataset with a customer ID, an order date, and an order value.
